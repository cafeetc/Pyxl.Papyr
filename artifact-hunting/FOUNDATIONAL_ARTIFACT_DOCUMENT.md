# Foundational Artifact Document

## §1. What Is an Artifact

### §1.1 — Framing: Out West

Artifacts are everywhere, not just film.

- **Book:** ink blots, creases, tears — just there, not the text
- **Painting:** remnants of an unrelated painting underneath, a hair caught in the paint
- **Film:** flakes of dust, deformations, rips and tears, acidic blotches, tape, markers, dead bugs

Film artifacts are different from book/painting artifacts. They are often inconsequential, singular pieces of a film, but they become the most intriguing still images when viewed in context, or even as a tiny loop.

We are operating at a somewhat subjective level where we can analyze these as parts of a film — some will do better at guessing than others, but they are still there.

https://archive.org/details/OutWest

The first 12 seconds is a masterclass of tape, specifically. At least three extremely notable artifacts not present in other prints.

### §1.2 — Definition

A film artifact is any non-diegetic material that appears on screen — anything that reminds us of its journey from an idea to reality, not counting the content itself.

Artifacts are evidence of the process behind the film.

Artifacts are a good thing. They are organic reminders of the depths that previous generations undertook to create something. Largely devoid of creative thought, but hardly artificial either.

This shows the importance of physical film.

### §1.3 — Describing it

The scene: a man lighting a cigar.

It is the intro to the movie in some prints, a midpoint in others. We can experience this scene in three different ways. The artifacts make each version distinct.

Artifacts are most noticeable in silent films.

### §1.4 — The story it tells, factually

One could write an entire story about this singular print of a singular shot in a singular film you've probably never seen.

Not that it's more exciting than the film, but it's a silent story of blemishes that arose and were dealt with, not deleted. They were specks of dust and proof of life.

Their disappearance largely coincides with film's pursuit of shiny objects at the expense of the creativity that was present in a world where a tear in the film was not an easy fix.

We want to return to the mindset of those who created these films, placed the artifacts, preserved them later on, but didn't try to cover it with slop. Just glue, tape, staples, paper, etc.

### §1.5 — The story it tells, as fiction

The fiction is the story we tell ourselves when we see it. Maybe it's part of the scene, maybe we think we know the origin. Maybe our mind just takes us somewhere else. That's the important part right there.

## §2. Content / Artifact Boundary

### §2.1 — Deliberate vs. Organic

A filmmaker who deliberately scratches the negative because they want a "distressed film" look — that is likely not an artifact. But if there is no knowledge of this action beyond that person, it's likely an artifact to the person viewing it. It is important to note that while we do not celebrate deliberate artifacts, it's ultimately interpretive.

The Out West tape was not put there to be cute, it was likely placed there to cover up damage that took away from that shot. Though some may see it differently.

Artifacts that are relics of editing, prints, and occasional digital issues are artifacts because they are non-diegetic, not creative choices.

### §2.2 — Medium choice and accidents

A director who shoots on grainy high-speed stock on purpose — yes, the grain is an artifact, because they ultimately chose a medium that leaves artifacts. If they artificially added grain, it is not.

A lab accident that creates a blur/freeze-frame, and the director keeps it — yes, this is a great artifact because they actually used the constraint life gave them and kept it in instead of eliminating its story.

### §2.3 — Content / artifact boundary

Motion blur is content unless it gets a ghost image or other artifact as a result.

Vignetting done artistically is not an artifact. Fading that happens over time or due to bad exposures are artifacts.

### §2.4 — Exceptions

Cigarette burns / reel change markers are absolutely artifact. It is an exception to the rule as it may be deliberate but it is also a necessary part of the process that often gets left in.

### §2.5 — Anachronistic artifacts

Digital artifacts — compression blocking, etc. — yes, these count, but they will be asterisked as "Anachronistic" artifacts. Not part of the process, but corruption etc. (non-purposeful) can absolutely create artifacts. We will only count those "burnt" into a file though. No deliberate pixelation.

The pixelation of an old video or small video file is an artifact the same way general dust, noise, blurs, etc. can be in old movies.

## §3. Tier System / Rarity

### §3.1 — The 7-point scale

| Tier | Label | Definition | Example Artifacts |
|------|-------|------------|-------------------|
| 1 | Atmospheric | Artifacts so common they resemble a storm or aura on the screen | Standard grain that permeates the whole thing |
| 2 | Common | On average, 3/5 frames have the same type/types of artifact | Cigarette burns; tape on scene transitions; dust; specks; tiny dots; scratches; hairs; larger/more noticeable grain; somewhat unique variants of grain |
| 3 | Slightly Uncommon | Well-contrasted artifacts | Solid blocks of decay; large blobs; Splice Tape (single piece, noticeable) |
| 4 | Uncommon | Extreme versions of Tier 3 artifacts. Bigger, darker, more noticeable, more contrasted | A blotch of decomposition, perhaps a bubble (Tier 3) → a more unique shape, perhaps several bulbs (Tier 4). A smaller, less discernible blotch = Tier 2 |
| 5 | Slightly Rare | Identifiable, peculiar artifacts. Not quite an amazing anomaly, but discernible from your uncommon ones | If an uncommon artifact is an especially large blotch, the slightly rare one is a cluster of those making a more unique shape, but it does not dominate the shot |
| 6 | Rare | Absolute extremes. Identifiable on their own. Likely dominates the shot, no matter the size or shape | A dead bug; an especially large square of tape; a giant X of splice tape |
| 7 | Unique / One of a Kind | S-Tier. A vibe check. Rare ones notable beyond this scope. The best of the best. A largely arbitrary way to set truly unique artifacts apart. Functionally Tier 6, but subjectively better | Out West tape — first 12 seconds, cigar-lighting scene. https://archive.org/details/OutWest |

### §3.2 — Tiering rules

- Primary axis: Intensity and rarity
- Frequency only matters for Atmospheric (Tier 1)
- Longevity (artifacts persisting over multiple frames) — rated like any artifact, just with a frame range
- Anachronistic (digital) artifacts — tiered on the same 1–7 scale, asterisked to connote digital

## §4. Artifact Types

11 artifact types, name + description + origin each, sourced to Sickboat (https://sickboat.com/blogs/blog/types-of-film-artifacts):

1. **Film Leader** — Head/tail leader strips for threading projectors/telecines
2. **Film Countdowns** — SMPTE universal leader, 8→2 countdown, "2-pop"
3. **Film Grain** — Silver halide texture, natural to the medium
4. **Film Dirt / Dust / Noise** — Physical foreign bodies on emulsion, not chemical texture
5. **Film Scratches** — Linear abrasions from mishandling / dirty gates
6. **Film Burns** — Light exposure on emulsion, edges of frame, various colors
7. **Film Flash Frames** — Like burns but shorter, 1–2 frames
8. **Film Splices** — Two stocks joined by tape/cement, visible join line
9. **Film Punch Holes** — Marker punched into stock for shot transitions
10. **Film Markers / Cinemarkers** — Hand-drawn marks on film stock (Sharpie etc.)
11. **Film Mattes / Aspect Ratio Mattes** — Frame edges, letterboxes, sprocket holes, gate marks

Types are separate from tier ratings — any type can appear at any tier.

## §5. External vs Internal

- **External** — something placed there by a person. Examples: dust, tape, marker.
- **Internal** — happened due to natural age, mishandling, or simple fluke accident. Examples: damage, melted film, bubbles, acid burns. Tears are internal — they are defects once they are there, causation does not matter.

## §7. Longevity

Start frame → end frame.

A scratch that is clearly on multiple frames counts as longevity. A long stretch of acid burns counts as longevity. Any persistence or chain reaction like that is longevity.

Same artifact across multiple frames = one artifact with longevity, not multiple artifacts.
